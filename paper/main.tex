\begin{abstract}
On-device generative inference usually means choosing one backend, and single-backend deployment leaves most of a heterogeneous SoC idle. We present \emph{Surgical Inference}: compile a generative model as a set of fixed-shape submodels and native stages, reshape each stage --- without changing its math --- until the engine that should run it admits it, and place every stage on the CPU, GPU or Neural Engine (ANE) by measurement.

On Kokoro-82M text-to-speech, the ANE rejects the vocoder, 89\% of the model's FLOPs, on tensor geometry. Folding its time axis into overlapping rows, rewriting each rejected op into an admitted equivalent and keeping exact global normalization statistics puts all but one of its 1,928 ops on the ANE. Splitting each vocoder stage so the ANE runs the convolutions while the GPU runs normalization and activations, over shared memory, keeps both engines busy at once. The pipeline synthesizes 27 s of speech in 309 ms on an M1 Mac Mini, 9.8$\times$ faster than MLX on the same machine, and in 319 ms on an iPhone 15 Pro Max, at 40.8--45.8 dB SNR against the fp32 reference. It needs about half of MLX's peak memory on the Mac and 2.4--19$\times$ less on iPhones, and on the iPhone stays thermally nominal while MLX, on the same work, reaches throttling. On Magenta RealTime 2, a streaming music model with a 40 ms frame deadline, the same procedure runs faster than real time on an iPhone 15 Pro Max in bounded runs, though not yet in a 10-minute soak, and finds two more structural admission rules: in-graph state mutation, not attention, is the ANE compile cliff, and tensor layout decides whether FP16 output stays finite on each engine.

Negative results are reported as results: no valid monolithic export, a list of optimizations that failed, and an estimated ceiling for the exact model below 90\% of the hardware floor. Code, models and benchmarks are open source.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} on-device inference, heterogeneous computing, Apple Silicon, Neural Engine, Core ML, model decomposition, text-to-speech, vocoder, autoregressive streaming

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Apple Silicon puts CPU cores, a GPU and a neural accelerator (the Apple Neural Engine, ANE) on one die with unified memory, and similar CPU/GPU/NPU designs now ship in most phones and laptops. Local generative inference nonetheless runs almost entirely on the GPU, through llama.cpp, MLX and PyTorch MPS. The GPU is programmable and forgiving, but a GPU-only model competes with rendering, media and any concurrent local LLM, while the ANE sits idle.

The obvious alternative --- compile the whole model for the ANE --- fails for most generative models. ANE compilers impose static shapes, an op set and per-axis tensor limits, and a generative model is rarely one homogeneous graph: it mixes dense convolutions, recurrence, data-dependent indexing, DSP and sampling. When the compiler rejects part of the graph, Core ML falls back silently to another engine, and the developer learns nothing.

We argue that the unit of deployment should be the inference pipeline, not the model file, and that a rejection by the accelerator is a statement about a tensor's \emph{shape}, not about the model. \emph{Surgical Inference} cuts a pipeline at its dataflow seams into fixed-shape Core ML submodels and native stages, reshapes stages the ANE rejects into equivalent forms it admits, and places each stage by measurement. We test it on two workloads chosen to be structural opposites: Kokoro-82M, a batch text-to-speech pipeline, and Magenta RealTime 2 (MRT2), an autoregressive music model that streams under a 40 ms per-frame deadline on a phone.

The paper's thesis emerged from the two case studies rather than preceding them. \textbf{In both workloads, what decided whether the ANE admitted a stage --- and whether its output stayed correct --- was structure the developer controls: tensor geometry, state boundaries and memory layout, not which ops the model uses.} Kokoro's vocoder was rejected on geometry and admitted after folding; MRT2's transformer was rejected for mutating state in-graph and admitted as a stateless step; MRT2's codec produced NaNs in one layout and correct audio in another. Op-level rewrites suggested by ANE folklore changed nothing in either.

We make five contributions:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{A decomposition methodology} for multi-stage generative pipelines: a three-motif classification (Sequential-Dynamic, Data-Dependent Logic, Dense-Static), a decomposition procedure with a per-stage placement ablation, and host-glue validation rules (\S\ref{sec:method}). It systematizes practice already visible in per-stage Core ML deployments such as Apple's Stable Diffusion port and WhisperKit (\S\ref{sec:related}); we make the procedure explicit and test it on two opposite workloads.

\item \textbf{Folding an audio-rate vocoder onto the ANE without changing its math} (\S\ref{sec:cs1-fold}). Kokoro's vocoder carries time axes of up to 720,000 elements against a 16,384-element per-axis limit. Folding time into rows with overlapping halos, five op rewrites and exact global AdaIN statistics make it ANE-resident and exact (68 dB against the unfolded generator in fp32).

\item \textbf{One stage, two engines} (\S\ref{sec:cs1-hybrid}). Measured per op class, the ANE is bound by the vocoder's elementwise work and the GPU by its convolutions. A hybrid generator runs every convolution on the ANE and normalization and activations in Metal, over shared IOSurface tensors, with both engines busy at once. It beats the all-ANE form by 16--78\% at every input of 7 s and longer on all five devices, at a measured cost in phone memory and GPU time (\S\ref{sec:cs1-fold-vs-hybrid}).

\item \textbf{Structural ANE admission findings} from the streaming case study (\S\ref{sec:cs2}): in-graph state mutation, not attention math, is the compile cliff for streaming transformers; internal layout decides FP16 numerical survival per compute unit; and weight-bound stages cost weight bytes $\div$ DRAM bandwidth per call on every engine.

\item \textbf{Measured results, a cumulative ablation and negative results} on three Macs and two iPhones under a frozen protocol (\S\ref{sec:cs1-eval}): a one-change-at-a-time ladder from the previous pipeline to the current one, peak memory and phone thermal state against MLX, a lab estimate of the remaining distance to the hardware floor, a monolithic-export control that fails, and the optimizations that did not work.
\end{enumerate}

Code, pre-converted Core ML models and benchmark data for Kokoro are at \url{https://github.com/mattmireles/kokoro-coreml} and \url{https://huggingface.co/mattmireles/kokoro-coreml}. MRT2's conversion and validation layer is at \url{https://github.com/mattmireles/magenta-realtime-2-iphone}; its streaming runtime lives in a fork that is private at the time of writing (Appendix~\ref{app:artifacts}).

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}

\textbf{The SoC.} Apple's M- and A-series chips share one DRAM pool among three engines with different admission rules and power profiles. The \emph{CPU} handles branching, dynamic shapes and DSP (Accelerate/vDSP). The \emph{GPU} runs arbitrary compute through Metal and handles dynamic shapes. The \emph{ANE} is a fixed-function accelerator for convolutions, matrix multiplies, elementwise ops, pooling and normalization, under strict constraints: static shapes, a restricted op set, no data-dependent control flow, and per-axis tensor limits (16,384 elements per axis, by community documentation~\citep{ane-field-guide,orion} and our own compiler errors). Core ML~\citep{coremltools} dispatches to all three, but a compute-unit setting is a \emph{request}: subgraphs the ANE rejects run elsewhere without an error.

\textbf{GPU-centric practice.} llama.cpp~\citep{llamacpp}, MLX~\citep{mlx} and PyTorch MPS~\citep{pytorch-mps-metal,pytorch-mps-notes} target only the CPU and GPU. The common view that Core ML offers little for generative inference is often right for large language models, which are bandwidth-bound; the ANE adds no bandwidth. It does not transfer to pipelines with separable dense, sequential and data-dependent regions.

\textbf{Kokoro-82M}~\citep{kokoro-model,kokoro-lib} is an Apache-2.0 text-to-speech model built on StyleTTS~2~\citep{styletts2} with an iSTFTNet-style vocoder~\citep{istftnet}. Its reference inference path has seven stages: duration prediction (a BERT encoder and an LSTM prosody predictor), duration-dependent expansion of tokens to frames, pitch and noise (F0/N) prediction, decoder pre-processing (\emph{DecoderPre}), a harmonic source (hn-nsf: SineGen plus STFT), the vocoder (\emph{generator}), and trim. The generator holds 1,348 of the model's 1,520 GFLOP for a 27 s utterance (89\%).

\textbf{Magenta RealTime 2}~\citep{magenta-rt,magenta-rt-repo} is Google's open-weights live-music model. A decoder-only transformer generates one frame of 12 residual-vector-quantized (RVQ) tokens every 40 ms, and a SpectroStream codec decoder in the SoundStream lineage~\citep{soundstream} turns each frame into 48 kHz stereo audio. Upstream real-time inference runs through MLX on M-series Macs only. We use \code{mrt2\_small} (230M parameters).

\section{Surgical Inference}\label{sec:method}

Surgical Inference changes the compiled artifact boundary, not the weights. A reference model becomes a few fixed-shape neural packages plus native code for dynamic or irregular work. The rules are short: cut only at real dataflow boundaries; preserve numerical equivalence at each cut; when the accelerator rejects a stage, change the stage's shape before giving up on the accelerator; let measured placement override intuition; and treat host glue as part of the model.

\subsection{Computational Motifs}\label{sec:motifs}

\textbf{Sequential-Dynamic (SD)}: recurrence, autoregressive decoding, sampling loops. Candidate engines: CPU or GPU. The motif belongs to the boundary drawn, not to the stage inherited: when the only recurrence is cache state, the per-step math is Dense-Static and the bookkeeping is host logic (\S\ref{sec:cs2-state}).

\textbf{Data-Dependent Logic (DDL)}: computation whose structure depends on tensor values --- duration expansion, masking, top-k, trimming. Candidate engine: native CPU code; too irregular for an accelerator to amortize.

\textbf{Dense-Static (DS)}: convolutions, matrix multiplies, normalization and elementwise ops with compile-time shapes. Candidate engines: Core ML, with placement measured. Dense-Static is the candidate set for the ANE, not a guarantee of admission (\S\ref{sec:disc-admission}).

\subsection{Procedure}\label{sec:procedure}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=\arabic*.]
\item Trace the reference forward pass; mark boundaries where data-dependent logic enters or leaves the graph, values stable enough to validate, and independent branches.
\item Classify each stage by motif.
\item \textbf{Dense-Static stages:} make shapes static (bucketing with masks), export to Core ML, and validate against a native-length fp32 reference across bucket fills, by SNR over the valid region (per-stage correlation on padded inputs proved too weak, \S\ref{sec:cs1-decomp}). If the ANE rejects the stage, find the binding structural property (\S\ref{sec:disc-admission}) and reshape: fold long axes into rows, move state to host-owned inputs and outputs, change internal layout. Then ablate placement per stage --- \code{.cpuAndNeuralEngine}, \code{.cpuAndGPU}, and splits within the stage by op class --- and keep the fastest variant that passes validation. Prove placement with \code{MLComputePlan} or Instruments; never trust \code{.all}.
\item \textbf{Data-Dependent stages:} implement natively (Swift/C, Accelerate). Profile them: a naive port is often slower than PyTorch.
\item \textbf{Sequential-Dynamic stages:} if the recurrence is only cache state, export a stateless step function with caches as inputs and updates as outputs. If the stage is weight-bound, expect per-call cost $\approx$ weight bytes $\div$ DRAM bandwidth on any engine and reduce calls before chasing placement (\S\ref{sec:cs2-bandwidth}).
\item Orchestrate natively. Overlap independent stages across engines where the dataflow proves it safe. Validate every host-side consumer of a model output against the reference at least once, read tensors through their strides, and prove that stateful graphs read their state (\S\ref{sec:cs2-host}).
\item Benchmark end to end against the original framework, GPU-native frameworks, other ports, and a monolithic conversion if one can be built (\S\ref{sec:cs1-monolith}), across input lengths and devices; change one thing at a time when attributing gains.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Host glue is part of the model.} Several of the largest costs we found were outside any model graph: a one-hot alignment implemented as a dense matrix multiply, boxed element reads over strided FP16 outputs (about 1 s per 30 s clip on an M1 until replaced by vectorized run conversion), and Core ML repacking pointer-backed inputs on every call, which doubled the per-call cost of ANE convolutions until the tensors were backed by IOSurfaces (\S\ref{sec:cs1-hybrid}). In MRT2, host glue silently destroyed correctness (\S\ref{sec:cs2-host}). End-to-end wall time is the primary metric; per-stage times are diagnostics.

\textbf{Export.} Packages are exported with coremltools 8.3~\citep{coremltools,coremltools-pytorch} in FP16. Duration and F0Ntrain ship at enumerated sizes with masks: the ANE rejects flexible shapes, an LSTM over a symbolic length ran about 5$\times$ slower on the CPU, and \code{EnumeratedShapes} cost about 140 ms whenever the shape changed. Replacing an unrolled bidirectional LSTM (up to 134,397 ops) with two stock \code{lstm} ops cut the padded duration stage from 82--733 ms to 11--45 ms on an M2 Ultra. Masking matters for correctness, not just speed: bucket padding had contaminated normalization statistics (one 2.8 s utterance forced through every bucket sat at 15 dB F0 SNR against a native-length run), which per-stage correlation above 0.9999 had not revealed. Current packages are scored by SNR over the valid region against fp32 PyTorch on real inputs.

\textbf{Native stages.} Alignment copies each token's vector into its predicted frame slots; no alignment matrix exists in the hot path. The harmonic source runs in Swift with vDSP: double-precision phase accumulation, phase increments computed per F0 frame instead of upsampling by 300 and back, bulk noise generation, and a cached windowed-DFT STFT. An early port was 4$\times$ slower than PyTorch until per-sample RNG calls through Swift's protocol dispatch were replaced (a 12$\times$ speedup); that and an absorbing zero state in its xorshift RNG are the kind of defect the procedure's profiling and validation steps exist to catch. hn-nsf cannot itself be exported: Core ML's execution of SineGen's cumulative phase gives correlation near zero.

\subsection{The Vocoder Wall}\label{sec:cs1-wall}

The generator's harmonic input has a last axis of 14,401--144,001 elements across buckets, and its waveform output 72,000--720,000. Loaded for the ANE, it fails compilation on every Mac we tested (\code{ANECCompile() FAILED}; \code{Tensor width goes beyond limit supported (16390 > 16384)}); under \code{.all}, macOS reroutes it silently, and in an April 2026 ablation the fallback ran 54$\times$ slower than CPU+GPU. Source rewrites suggested by ANE reverse-engineering literature~\citep{orion,ane-field-guide} --- \code{Linear} to 1$\times$1 convolution, native instance-norm lowering, broadcast AdaIN, FP16 inputs, palettization --- all validated and none changed runtime. The October 2026 pipeline therefore ran the generator on the GPU, where at 30 s it took 88.5\% of end-to-end time on the M2 Air and 92.7\% on the A14 (R0 in Table~\ref{tab:cs1-stages}). laishere/kokoro-coreml~\citep{laishere} splits the vocoder into several Core ML packages; under \code{.cpuAndNeuralEngine} on an M1, \code{MLComputePlan} assigns about 47.5\% of its estimated vocoder cost to the ANE and the rest to the CPU.

\subsection{Folding the Vocoder onto the ANE}\label{sec:cs1-fold}

The limit is on one axis's length, not on the work. The generator is a stack of 1-D convolutions with a receptive field of about 8 \code{x\_pre} frames, so its time axis can be cut into rows that the ANE processes in a batch axis. Each row carries \code{core} frames plus a \code{halo} of 8 frames on each side; outputs are taken from the core and stitched back. Table~\ref{tab:cs1-fold-ops} lists what else had to change; every rewrite is exact in fp32 (68 dB against the unfolded generator).

Two numerical problems surfaced only on the ANE. First, some channels have mean $\approx$3 and standard deviation $\approx$0.08; the ANE's FP16 masked mean was about $10^{-3}$ relative off (the GPU's $2\times10^{-4}$), shifting whole channels by 0.04$\sigma$ and holding the first build to 38.7 dB. Two-pass centring ($c = x - \bar x$; $c = c - \bar c$) fixed it. A pre-scaling meant to avoid FP16 overflow instead pushed small variances into subnormals, which the ANE flushes to zero, and the output fell to 16 dB --- visible only when blocks were composed. Second, AdaIN statistics must be exact and global: estimating them from every $k$-th row, which would have allowed streaming rows with bounded memory, gave 1--4 dB for every $k$ from 2 to 10.

The frozen folded package (polynomial Snake, 30 s) places 1,927 of its 1,928 ops on the ANE of both an M2 Air and an A14, and scores 40.8--44.1 dB against the fp32 PyTorch generator on the frozen test clips; the native-\code{sin} variant, which the runtime picks only on A17-class ANEs, leaves 96 \code{sin} and multiply ops on the CPU of an M2. Admission did not make it fast everywhere. In the rerun's ablation the folded generator beat the GPU generator on the A14 (30 s pipeline: 1,556 against 3,679 ms) and on the M2 Air (821 against 1,091 ms), but on the M2 Ultra, whose GPU is large, the folded pipeline took 706 ms at 30 s, against 373 ms for the October GPU pipeline under similar load in another session (287 ms in its quiet pass). Admission was necessary, not sufficient.

\subsection{One Stage, Two Engines}\label{sec:cs1-hybrid}

Ablating the folded graph op class by op class explains why. In lab runs on earlier builds, on an M1 ANE Snake activations took about 43\% of the generator's time, AdaIN 23\% and the convolutions 23\%; on the A17 Pro, the convolutions alone ran in 514 ms on the ANE and 1,677 ms on the GPU. The ANE is bound by the vocoder's elementwise work; the GPU by its convolutions. The engines have complementary weaknesses inside one stage.

The hybrid generator assigns each op class to the engine that is fast at it. The ANE runs every convolution, as 53 small Core ML functions per row count; Metal kernels run AdaIN statistics, the affine, Snake and the branch joins. Three engineering points made it work:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Shared memory without repacking.} With pointer-backed \code{MLMultiArray} inputs, Core ML repacked tensors on every call and an ANE convolution took 10.2 ms on an M1; backed by IOSurface pixel buffers, which Metal reads in place, it took 5.7 ms. The ``wake-up'' penalty we had attributed to ANE power gating was this copy.
\item \textbf{Layouts both engines like.} Dilated convolutions read and write rows phase-major (sample $q\cdot d + p$ stored at $p\cdot W/d + q$), so on the ANE they become plain convolutions, and the Metal passes convert layout inside work they already do. Vectorizing those passes to \code{half4} brought them to about the memory-bandwidth floor.
\item \textbf{Overlap.} Independent chains keep the ANE and GPU busy at once: on the A14, a 30 s generator call on the frozen build spans 375 ms with the ANE busy for 292 ms and the GPU for 268 ms, both at once for 192 ms.
\end{itemize}

The hybrid scores 40.8--45.8 dB; its worst clip, a 1.2 s ``Hi.'', runs on the folded generator. It serves buckets of 7 s and longer; at 3 s its roughly 100 ANE/GPU round trips lose to the single folded call. If a Metal command buffer fails --- as when an iOS app is backgrounded and loses GPU access --- the executor re-runs that clip on the folded generator.

\subsection{Evaluation}\label{sec:cs1-eval}

\textbf{Hardware.} M1 Mac Mini (16 GB, 68 GB/s), M2 MacBook Air (24 GB, 100 GB/s), M2 Ultra Mac Studio (64 GB, 800 GB/s), iPhone 12 Pro (A14, 6 GB) and iPhone 15 Pro Max (A17 Pro, 8 GB). The M2 Ultra ran macOS 26.7.1, the M2 Air and M1 macOS 27.0.1, and both iPhones iOS 27.0.1.

\textbf{Inputs and boundary.} Five frozen inputs targeting the runtime buckets (2.80, 6.75, 9.60, 13.90 and 27.38 s of audio), voice \code{af\_heart}, speed 1.0. Timing runs from token IDs and voice embedding in to a 24 kHz waveform in memory (Appendix~\ref{app:boundaries}).

\textbf{Protocol} (plan 014, fixed before the run, Appendix~\ref{app:artifacts}; deviations below). Release builds. Each Surgical cell discards three preflight calls, records one cold call, and reports the median of ten warm calls; the Mac MLX harness records one cold call and ten warm calls with no preflight. Macs: plugged in, fleet worker paused, and each arm waits for the one-minute load average to fall to 1.5. No Mac reached that gate (one-minute load 2--7 from background apps, with no fleet jobs running), so every Mac row is an upper bound. iPhones: one app launch per arm per bucket, a cooldown before each launch, a nominal thermal state at launch, thermal state recorded per call, and arm order alternated by bucket. Six cells ended above nominal and are marked where they appear: the A14's R0, R1 and R2 cells at 30 s (old generator) and the A17's MLX cells at 10, 15 and 30 s. \emph{Deviations,} made mid-run to finish in one day: the phone cooldown fell from ten minutes to three (six for the A17's last six cells) and the Mac gate wait from 20 minutes to two. On the A14, the folded 3 s cell at three minutes took 98.5 ms against 92.0 and 98.2 ms for two same-pipeline cells at ten minutes, so the A14's launch-to-launch noise is at least 6.7\%; the A17 had no clean ten-minute reference. The A17's first cells, taken while the phone was in use, were discarded and rerun, and the iPhone app was rebuilt once to restore MLX's model files, with Core ML code and packages unchanged. Phones are reported in this sustained regime; a single request after idle is slower (\S\ref{sec:cs1-limits}). MLX runs in the same session as the Surgical pipeline. Raw per-call timings, package hashes, commit and host state for every cell are archived (Appendix~\ref{app:artifacts}).

\textbf{Configurations.} \emph{Surgical} is the shipped pipeline (Table~\ref{tab:cs1-placement}) with its default generator, hybrid (folded below 7 s), chosen by a rule fixed before the run: hybrid becomes the default only if it is faster than folded on every device, at equal quality, with no crash in the matrix. \emph{MLX} is mlx-audio~\citep{mlx-audio} 0.4.3 (commit \code{862dfbe}) on Macs and mlalma/kokoro-ios~\citep{kokoro-ios} 1.0.8 on iPhones. Only public Core ML APIs are used, except in the one column labeled \emph{e5rt} (\S\ref{sec:cs1-e5rt}).

\subsubsection{Where the gain comes from: a cumulative ablation}\label{sec:cs1-ablation}

\subsubsection{How close to the hardware?}\label{sec:cs1-ceiling}

We estimate a floor for the 30 s input from first principles: every convolution and matrix multiply on the ANE at its measured marginal rate (from per-function fits $t = a + b\cdot\text{GFLOP}$: M1 $\approx$9.8 TFLOPS, A14 4.2--5.2, A17 Pro 7--9), elementwise and AdaIN traffic hidden under it on the GPU, and the sequential LSTMs on the CPU ($\approx$30 ms). Because the hybrid moves about 8 GB through DRAM per 30 s call, a second floor takes the maximum of the compute time and bytes $\div$ bandwidth. We have not recomputed the floors against the frozen build; in lab runs on the same hybrid generator the pipeline sat at roughly 62--80\% of them. The remaining gap is mostly a fixed cost per ANE call that is I/O rather than dispatch (fusing three convolutions into one call saved 0.5--0.85 ms per removed call; a k3 convolution plus residual moves about 117 MB in 6 ms on the A14), plus fold overhead (1.78 vs 1.35 TFLOP, from halos and row padding) and ANE/GPU hand-offs. We estimate the exact model cannot reach much above 75--85\% of the floor with public APIs; exceeding 90\% would require moving fewer bytes than the model does, for example a distilled generator, which would break the exactness contract. These are estimates, not measurements.

\subsubsection{The private API buys almost nothing}\label{sec:cs1-e5rt}

Core ML's own calls into Espresso's private \code{e5rt} C API can be traced and replayed directly, from a command-line tool or a sandboxed iOS app, with output bit-identical to Core ML. In lab measurements it saves at most about 0.2 ms per ANE call (A14 k3 convolution: 5.91 ms Core ML, 5.70 ms e5rt), because the calls are bound by ANE I/O, which the private path does not change. In the rerun it lowered peak memory at 30 s by 15--23\% on the M2 Ultra and the phones (A14: 643 against 831 MB) and by 3--4\% on the M2 Air and M1, and it did not reliably lower latency: at 7 s and longer, e5rt was slower on the A17 Pro and 32--64\% slower on the M2 Ultra, within 6\% of the public path on the A14 and M1, and 39--60\% faster at 10--30 s only on the M2 Air, the noisiest host. An app that uses it cannot ship in the App Store and may break with any OS update; we report it in one labeled column (Table~\ref{tab:cs1-iphone}) and use only the public path for every claim. The SDK compiles it out by default.

\subsubsection{Control: no valid monolithic export}\label{sec:cs1-monolith}

The null hypothesis against decomposition is one Core ML package for the whole model. With coremltools 8.3 it fails at two seams the pipeline already cuts. The full graph does not convert: duration-driven \code{repeat\_interleave} has no static lowering (\code{ValueError: Cannot add const [None]}), and \code{torch.jit.trace} ``succeeds'' only by baking one utterance's alignment into constants --- the traced graph crashes on a same-shape input with different durations. The strongest partial monolith, everything downstream of alignment, converts and produces wrong audio: correlation 0.27 in FP16 and 0.25 in FP32 against PyTorch, from Core ML's execution of SineGen's cumulative phase. For this model, decomposition was the precondition for any correct Core ML artifact we could build.

\subsubsection{Audio quality}\label{sec:cs1-quality}

Every package is gated on SNR over the valid region against fp32 PyTorch; the frozen generators score 40.8--44.1 dB (folded) and 40.8--45.8 dB (hybrid) per clip against the fp32 PyTorch generator on the same inputs, above the 40 dB gate fixed before the run. The worst clip is the shortest input, ``Hi.'' (1.2 s); numbers score 44.1/45.8 dB and heavy punctuation 43.4/44.9 dB (folded/hybrid). The split duration model predicts the same frame counts as the unsplit one on all eight clips. SNR alone has failed us before: in July 2026 a blind model-judged listening evaluation (Gemini, paired lineups with a known-bad control) caught three defects that every numerical gate had passed --- a post-process that silenced real speech near punctuation, an inverse STFT at half amplitude for interior bins, and an RNG with an absorbing zero state. For the frozen build, the author listened to five clips (seed 42) covering the longest bucket, numbers, heavy punctuation, the shortest input and the folded generator, and heard no artifacts; per-clip hashes are archived (Appendix~\ref{app:artifacts}). The plan called for the same automated judge; its cloud project failed on billing (HTTP 403), so the author listened instead. This is one unblinded listener, not a rated or blind study, and not a human MOS evaluation.

\subsection{Limitations of Case Study 1}\label{sec:cs1-limits}

\textbf{Regime.} Phone numbers are sustained (three warm-ups, then ten back-to-back calls). A single request after idle is slower: the A14 generator pays a cold penalty after any break from its own work, which we could not remove with public or private APIs. Short bursts are faster than the sustained median. The rerun's ``cold'' call follows three preflight calls and lands within a few percent of the warm median (A14 hybrid 30 s: 485 vs 480 ms); it does not measure the after-idle penalty, which we did not re-measure on the frozen build.

\textbf{OS state.} The flexible-length GPU generator of the October pipeline can abort inside MPSGraph on macOS 27.0.1; it is disabled on OS 27 and not used here.

\textbf{No concurrent-workload benchmark.} We have not measured synthesis while a local LLM uses the GPU. In a lab run on an earlier build, a GPU blit kernel moving 51 GB/s on an M1 slowed the hybrid generator by 34\%; the blit also takes GPU time, so this mixes DRAM and GPU contention, and the frozen build has not been measured under load.

\textbf{Coverage.} One vendor; M1, M2 and M2 Ultra Macs; A14 and A17 Pro phones.

\section{Case Study 2: Magenta RealTime 2 Under a 40 ms Deadline}\label{sec:cs2}

MRT2 is Kokoro's structural complement. Kokoro renders a finished utterance once; MRT2 generates one frame every 40 ms indefinitely, frame $N+1$ depends on frame $N$'s tokens, and success is a sustained zero-underrun stream with p99 frame time under the deadline. It also has the motif Kokoro could route around: a stateful transformer (12 temporal layers, 41-frame local-attention K/V caches) plus a 12-level serial depth sampler on the hot path.

\textbf{Pipeline and setup.} Per frame: host conditioning $\rightarrow$ Core ML temporal step $\rightarrow$ Core ML depth rollout (all 12 RVQ levels in one prediction) $\rightarrow$ host codebook summation $\rightarrow$ Core ML codec decoder $\rightarrow$ host iSTFT and overlap-add $\rightarrow$ lock-free ring $\rightarrow$ \code{AVAudioSourceNode} (Figure~\ref{fig:mrt2-pipeline}). Audio-rate tensors never enter a Core ML graph: the codec stops at STFT-rate features --- the geometry problem Kokoro's vocoder had to be folded to escape is designed out here. Measurements are on the same two iPhones, iOS 26.5, after numerical-parity gates against the MLX reference (transformer correlation $>$ 0.9999, codec SNR $\ge$ 115 dB); placement claims come from \code{MLComputePlan} and Instruments, never from the requested policy.

\subsection{State Mutation, Not Attention, Is the ANE Cliff}\label{sec:cs2-state}

Core ML's mutable state (\code{MLState}) holding the K/V caches fails twice. A one-frame stateful step compiles with an ANE island, but every multi-frame unroll fails ANE compilation (\code{ANECCompile() FAILED}, error $-$14) on both phones; and the one-frame graph that compiles is slower under streaming pressure than the same graph on the CPU (p99 29.7 vs 14.7 ms on the A14). A first host-owned-cache export that still assembled updates in-graph also failed. ``Streaming transformers don't fit the ANE'' was the available conclusion.

Instead we ran a falsification ladder on the A14: strip the graph to one suspect mechanism, prove it on device, add the next back (Table~\ref{tab:cs2-ladder}, Figure~\ref{fig:ane-ladder}).

Admission is also fragile in a way the ladder does not show. The stateless artifact that ran a 10-minute zero-underrun stream in a headless host later fell back to the CPU ($\approx$640 ms/frame) inside the full app, and the numerically correct rolling-cache graph that replaced it during a correctness overhaul (\S\ref{sec:cs2-host}) mutates state in-graph and fails on the A14, consistent with the finding. The temporal stage therefore ships on \code{.cpuAndGPU} today; placement must be re-proven in the shipping process, not per artifact.

Channels-first layout is documented vendor guidance for ANE throughput~\citep{apple-ane-transformers}. What we have not seen documented is the numerical coupling: \textbf{the ANE was the only engine that produced finite output from the same FP16 artifact.} Our hypothesis is that each engine lowers the same MIL program differently; we did not inspect the lowerings.

\subsection{Weight Bandwidth Sets the Graph Boundary}\label{sec:cs2-bandwidth}

The depth transformer samples 12 RVQ levels serially per frame. Twelve small predictions per frame cost about 40--45 ms on the A14, over the whole budget, whether each call computes twelve positions or one. Every prediction streams the stage's weights from DRAM: 97 MB $\div$ 3.4--3.8 ms $\approx$ 26--29 GB/s, the A14's bandwidth; the 364 MB temporal graph gives the same rate. \textbf{Per-call cost $\approx$ weight bytes $\div$ DRAM bandwidth}, the ordinary roofline bound, and since all engines share one DRAM no placement escapes it. Moving the 12-level rollout inside one prediction --- with host-supplied Gumbel noise~\citep{astar-sampling} and inverse temperature so sampling stays host-seeded and deterministic --- streams the weights once per frame: 37 ms FP32 $\rightarrow$ \textbf{12.7 ms FP16 on the A14, 8.4 ms on the A17 Pro}. The FP32 export is token-exact (0/900 mismatches); FP16 flips near-tie tokens and shipped after listening gates. The same bound reappears in Kokoro: the hybrid generator's ANE calls are bound by I/O, not by dispatch (\S\ref{sec:cs1-ceiling}).

\subsection{Host Glue Can Destroy Correctness}\label{sec:cs2-host}

Three bugs shipped in early device builds while every model-level validation passed. Core ML pads output rows for 64-byte alignment, and a Swift sampler that indexed by \code{count / 12} read level $k$'s logits $10k$ positions off --- coarse structure sounded plausible, fine detail was scrambled. The runtime never wrote the temporal feedback input and made one depth prediction per frame instead of twelve. And a stateful graph's baked attention bias masked all 41 history slots, so it wrote state every frame and never read it; parity tests pass on write-only state. These are the source of the procedure's rules (step 6): validate every host consumer of a model output, read through strides, and prove a stateful graph reads its state.

\subsection{Placement, Power and Status}\label{sec:cs2-power}

In the earlier all-ANE configuration, Instruments traces on both phones show ANE intervals for all three model families and no app-attributed GPU intervals, encoded as a machine-checked placement artifact. Paired Power Profiler captures on the A14, varying only the temporal stage's policy, show process GPU impact 0.000 and 48.1 B CPU instructions over 60 s for ANE, against 2.23 and 110.3 B for the GPU control (Figure~\ref{fig:power}); the ANE arm slept 43\% of the run under backpressure. These captures predate the correctness fixes: both arms ran identical semantics, so the comparison stands, but absolute numbers do not transfer.

\textbf{Status, plainly.} With the corrected pipeline (temporal on GPU, depth rollout FP16 on CPU, decoder on ANE), the iPhone 15 Pro Max runs faster than real time: about 29 ms/frame p50, zero underruns over bounded 45 s runs, 1.37$\times$ headroom. The 10-minute foreground soak fails on the A17 Pro: after banking about 20 s of lookahead, it throttles to about 0.95$\times$ real time around minutes 5--7. The named fixes --- the temporal stack back on the ANE via the stateless boundary, and fewer temporal weight bytes --- follow from the findings above and are not yet demonstrated.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Structure Decides Admission}\label{sec:disc-admission}

Across the two workloads, three structural properties governed ANE admission, and we found none of them documented as such by the vendor:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Tensor geometry} (\S\ref{sec:cs1-wall}--\ref{sec:cs1-fold}): audio-rate axes exceed per-axis limits. Folding the axis into rows, with exact global statistics, admitted the whole vocoder. MRT2 designed the problem out by stopping its codec at feature rate.
\item \textbf{In-graph state mutation} (\S\ref{sec:cs2-state}): every graph that mutated K/V state inside Core ML failed compilation; the same math as a stateless step compiled and beat the CPU and GPU.
\item \textbf{Layout-coupled FP16 numerics} (\S\ref{sec:cs2-layout}): one FP16 program was finite only on the ANE, and only after a channels-first rewrite.
\end{enumerate}

Op-level rewrites --- \code{Linear} to 1$\times$1 convolution, normalization lowerings --- changed nothing in either workload; the MIL compiler makes those choices itself. The developer's levers are shapes, state boundaries and layout, and only a per-stage on-device ablation reveals which one is binding. Admission is also not the end of the question: the admitted all-ANE vocoder was 2.2--4.4$\times$ slower than the hybrid at 30 s on every device, and on the M2 Ultra slower than October's GPU pipeline, until the stage was split by op class across both engines (\S\ref{sec:cs1-hybrid}). ``Dense-Static'' is the candidate set for an engine, and op-class cost inside a stage can still favor splitting it.

\subsection{What Did Not Work}\label{sec:disc-negative}

Failures shaped the pipeline as much as successes. In Kokoro: a monolithic export (\S\ref{sec:cs1-monolith}); op-level ANE rewrites (\S\ref{sec:cs1-wall}); sampled AdaIN statistics (1--4 dB); FP16 pre-scaling of AdaIN variance (16 dB, subnormal flush); fusing the three parallel convolution branches into one ANE call (0.5--0.85 ms saved per call, at the cost of ANE/GPU overlap); \code{.fastPrediction} specialization (no change); private \code{e5rt} dispatch (about 0.2 ms per call); QoS and work-interval settings, pre-warm loops and GPU-to-ANE event chaining (no gain); moving DecoderPre or the LSTMs to the GPU (slower, or changed duration rounding and therefore output); splitting a long first sentence for earlier audio (switching generators cost more than it saved); and halos below 8 frames. In MRT2: \code{MLState} caches, twelve depth predictions per frame, and FP32 or channels-last decoders.

\subsection{When Surgical Inference Is Worth It}\label{sec:disc-worth}

The method costs real engineering: per-bucket exports with validation, native DSP, per-stage ablations, a disciplined benchmark harness. It pays when the workload is compute-bound and has distinct stages (vocoders, codecs, image decoders, speech encoders), when it must run across a range of devices --- the margin is largest on base chips and phones, where GPU frameworks fail outright (in October 2026, PyTorch MPS failed the 15 s and 30 s inputs on every Mac and kokoro-ios was killed for memory on the 6 GB iPhone 12 Pro) --- and when the GPU is needed for something else. It pays least for large language models, whose cost is weight bandwidth (\S\ref{sec:cs2-bandwidth}), and for small models where framework overhead is already negligible.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:disc-limitations}

\textbf{Single vendor.} The motifs and procedure do not assume Core ML, but the admission catalog is Apple-specific; other NPU toolchains (QNN, LiteRT delegates) need their own ablations.

\textbf{Instrumentation asymmetry.} MRT2 has Instruments residency and paired power captures; Kokoro has \code{MLComputePlan} placement evidence on a Mac and on-device compute-plan placement on the A14, but no Instruments residency traces and no power captures yet.

\textbf{Quality evidence is narrow.} Both case studies rest on numerical gates plus listening checks: automated blind model-judged lineups with known-bad controls during development, and, for Kokoro's frozen build, one unblinded listener. Neither is a human MOS study.

\textbf{Two workloads.} ``Structure decides admission'' holds in both workloads we studied. It is an empirical regularity across two models, not a law.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Per-stage Core ML deployment.} Apple's Stable Diffusion port~\citep{apple-stable-diffusion} ships separate packages per model, and Apple's ANE transformer guidance~\citep{apple-ane-transformers} documents channels-first layout for throughput, which \S\ref{sec:cs2-layout} extends to numerical survival. whisper.cpp~\citep{whispercpp} runs Whisper's encoder through Core ML beside a Metal decoder, and WhisperKit~\citep{whisperkit} ships Whisper as per-stage Core ML models with per-stage compute units. Surgical Inference systematizes this practice; it adds the reshaping step for rejected stages, the admission findings that make per-stage ablation necessary, intra-stage ANE/GPU co-execution, and negative results.

\textbf{Kokoro ports.} laishere/kokoro-coreml~\citep{laishere} splits Kokoro's vocoder into several Core ML packages with partial ANE placement (\S\ref{sec:cs1-wall}); soniqo/speech-swift~\citep{speech-swift} ships a Core ML Kokoro with a fixed-length output; mlalma/kokoro-ios~\citep{kokoro-ios} is the MLX Swift port we compare against. Piper~\citep{piper} and MeloTTS~\citep{melotts} target CPU inference; we did not benchmark them.

\textbf{Reverse-engineering the ANE.} Orion~\citep{orion} and community guides~\citep{ane-field-guide} document per-axis limits, per-dispatch costs and preferred lowerings. We corroborate the geometry limit, find that their op-level rewrites do not change Core ML runtime, and add folding as an exact escape from the limit, the state-mutation cliff and layout-dependent FP16 survival.

\textbf{Heterogeneous scheduling.} StarPU~\citep{starpu} and Legion~\citep{legion} map tasks across CPU/GPU systems; ExecuTorch~\citep{executorch} and LiteRT delegates partition graphs across mobile backends at runtime. Closest to our hybrid generator, $\mu$Layer~\citep{mulayer} splits single layers across a phone's CPU and GPU, CoDL~\citep{codl} co-executes inference on CPU and GPU, and Band~\citep{band} coordinates multiple DNNs across mobile processors; for LLMs, llm.npu~\citep{llmnpu}, PowerInfer-2~\citep{powerinfer2} and HeteroLLM~\citep{heterollm} split inference across a phone's NPU, GPU and CPU. These systems mostly target Android SoCs with programmable access to each processor. We work through Core ML, whose ANE admission is opaque and must be probed, and split a stage by op class between an NPU and a GPU over shared memory.

\textbf{Live music on device.} MRT2's upstream path is MLX on M-series Macs~\citep{magenta-rt,magenta-rt-repo}. We know of no prior real-time phone deployment of this model class, nor prior documentation of the state-mutation cliff; Apple's \code{MLState} documentation~\citep{mlstate} does not specify ANE behavior for graphs that mutate state.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The ANE rejected Kokoro's vocoder, and the rejection turned out to be about one axis's length. Folding that axis into rows, without changing the math, put the vocoder on the ANE; splitting each stage by op class between the ANE and the GPU made it fast. The result synthesizes 27 s of speech in 309 ms on an M1 Mac Mini, 9.8$\times$ faster than MLX on the same machine, and in 319 ms on an iPhone 15 Pro Max, with about half of MLX's memory on the Mac and without warming the phone, where MLX on the same work reached thermal throttling. The same procedure carried a 230M-parameter streaming music model onto a phone, faster than real time in bounded runs though not yet in a 10-minute soak, and found the same pattern twice more: state mutation, not attention, was the compile cliff, and layout decided whether FP16 output was finite.

The useful question for on-device AI is not ``which accelerator should run this model?'' It is ``what is the smallest set of static packages and native kernels --- each reshaped until its engine admits it --- that lets the whole chip do the work?''
